Ordinary People, Extraordinary Places

The horror hits harder when the character could be sitting next to you on the train.

I’ve never been especially interested in heroes who were born ready. Give me the bloke who’s late for work, the woman who’s only on the expedition because the money’s good, the kid who’s just trying to get through the night without becoming a statistic. Put them somewhere they were never meant to go — a tear in the sky, a mine that shouldn’t open, a street that doesn’t belong to this century — and watch what happens.

That’s the engine under most of what I write. Not because ordinary people are boring, but because they’re the opposite. They’re the ones with mortgages and sore feet and a phone full of unanswered texts. When the world breaks around them, you feel the break. You don’t need a prophecy. You need a commute that never arrives in London.

Stephen King taught me that early — ordinary towns, ordinary rooms, something wrong humming under the floorboards. I never shook it. Science fiction and fantasy gave me the other half: the permission to take those same people and drop them somewhere the maps don’t cover. Horror is what happens when they try to stay human anyway.

If you’re writing in this space, I’d say start with who, not what. Get the person right. Get their job, their stubbornness, the thing they’d rather not admit they’re afraid of. Then open the door. The Scrapyard didn’t begin with aliens. It began with a train full of people who thought they were going home.

That’s still where I start every time. The person first. The place after. The fear finds its own way in.
